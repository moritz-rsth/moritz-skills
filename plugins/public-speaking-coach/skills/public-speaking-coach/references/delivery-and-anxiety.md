# Delivery, Presence, and Anxiety

## Managing speaking anxiety — five steps

1. **Just do it** — treat speaking like a physical exercise you build by repeating it. Every conversation can be practice; look for chances to insert a story or line from your speech file into everyday talk, and come prepared to situations where you might be asked to speak.
2. **Identify the anxiety specifically** — is it fear of failure, of strangers, of a specific past embarrassment? Naming it precisely (e.g., "this comes from stuttering while reading aloud in class once") makes it addressable instead of a vague dread.
3. **Visualize success, not failure** — don't rehearse the ways it could go wrong; picture the good version. The audience is not the enemy — in almost every real situation, they want the speaker to do well.
4. **Control the physical signs of nervousness** — redirect nervous energy into enthusiasm rather than suppressing it. A practical trick: wipe your hands dry and take one deliberate breath right before starting, and build a habit of linking that breath to the beginning of the speech.
5. **Practice, repeatedly** — with enough repetition, speaking starts to produce a rush of energy rather than dread, to the point where you can actually play with the audience instead of just surviving the talk.

## Presence and eye contact

- **Zen eye contact** — genuine contact with as many individuals as possible, not scanning or staring at one spot.
- **Captivate despite distractions** — don't add distracting movement or let the environment pull focus.
- **Charismatic without overdoing it** — don't exaggerate a bit or mannerism to the point it becomes the story.
- **Move the audience somewhere** — keep a clear throughline so it's obvious where the speech is headed at any point, not just a string of points.

## The five imperatives of holding a room (general delivery principles)

1. **Never say "I'm sorry" mid-speech** — apologizing (for time, for a mistake, for the material) costs attention and undercuts the performance. Grade yourself an A going in — improve for next time, don't apologize in the moment.
2. **Master the first impression** — see the opener guidance in `openers-and-structure.md`.
3. **Hyperfocus on the audience** — see eye contact/presence above.
4. **Add variety** — see the variety section in `openers-and-structure.md`.
5. **End before expected** — see the closing guidance in `openers-and-structure.md`.

## Reading the room in real time

Watch for the signs a room is losing attention — wandering eyes, shifting in seats, yawning — and adjust rather than pushing through the planned material unchanged. If the room is bored, re-engage (a question, a shift in pace, a callback); if energy has dropped hard, it's worth naming it lightly rather than pretending not to notice.
