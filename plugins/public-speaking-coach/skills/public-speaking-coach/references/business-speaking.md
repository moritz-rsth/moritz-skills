# Business Speaking: Pitches, Meetings, and Q&A

Relevant for pitches, sales talks, status updates, fundraising asks, and running meetings — common territory for an internship context (team updates, project pitches to senior staff, client-facing talks).

## Persuasive / sales structure

**Set a clear thesis first**: state the point plainly, e.g. "our product solves a real customer need and pain point," or "this project reduces the desk's reconciliation time by 40%." Everything else supports this one sentence.

**Appeal to values, not just features**: connect what you're proposing to something the audience already cares about — security, flexibility, time saved, credibility, risk reduction. A feature list persuades less than a value connection.

**Choose ascending or descending order for the body** based on the audience — descending (biggest point first) if you have limited attention/time, ascending (build to it) if you can count on them staying with you.

Two structures to choose between:

1. **Problem-Solution** — state the problem, illustrate the pain concretely, then present the solution and the resulting benefit.
2. **Motivational Sequence** — five steps: (1) get attention, (2) illustrate the need, (3) show the need can be satisfied, (4) let the audience visualize the satisfied state, (5) motivate them to take the specific action that gets them there.

Support every point along the way with logos, ethos, or pathos (see `openers-and-structure.md` for the state-demonstrate-restate pattern).

**Overcoming resistance/objections**:
- **Identification** — identify yourself with the audience's situation ("I've sat where you're sitting").
- **Directions** — tell them directly what to do: "I want you to approve this."
- **Suggestions** — softer framing: "I think this is the right approach because..."
- **Counter-persuasion** — acknowledge the opposing argument, then explain why it's outweighed. Don't pretend objections don't exist; naming them builds credibility.
- **Broadening** — widen a narrow topic to show broader relevance ("this isn't just a process fix, it changes how the desk reports risk").

## Running a meeting (the seven imperatives)

1. **Start and end on time** — don't wait for latecomers; introduce them briefly if they arrive after you've started.
2. **Have a written agenda** — explain why the meeting exists and how long each topic will take.
3. **Make it engaging** — even a dry topic benefits from energy; people show up more enthusiastic when the room has some.
4. **Focus** — moderate discussion actively, don't let it drift.
5. **Understand group dynamics** — ask quiet people directly what they think (they often have the best-informed opinions); the loudest voice in the room isn't automatically the best one.
6. **End early** — meetings default to running over; deliberately counter that.
7. **Take charge, even without formal authority** — useful phrases: "Have we put this on the agenda yet?" / "Before we spend more time on this, can we finish the remaining items?"

## Fundraising / asks (adaptable to any "ask" conversation — budget, headcount, sponsorship)

In-person asks depend on genuine personal connection, not just the pitch itself.

1. **Visualize the goal upfront** — introduce everyone in the room; never apologize for making the ask.
2. **Make the first contact to set up time**: "This is [name], calling on behalf of [group] to talk about [project]. I'd like about fifteen minutes to discuss it — do you have time next Wednesday at 7:30?"
3. **Open with small talk** to warm the relationship before getting to the ask.
4. **Ask for more than you need, confidently** — it recalibrates what the other side expects and rarely backfires. Softening language like "we were hoping..." makes the ask land less bluntly without weakening it.
5. **Handle a delay response**: "When would you like me to follow up on this?" / "Were you thinking about a specific range?"
6. **Handle a negative response**: "What number are you more comfortable with?" — keep the conversation open rather than closing on the first no.

## Making information memorable / teaching a point

Useful whenever the speech needs to actually transfer knowledge, not just persuade or entertain (training a new team, explaining a process).

- **Simplify the complex** — open by framing the topic as approachable; if the audience believes upfront that they can't learn it, they won't. Carry everyone along rather than leaving people behind early.
- **Teach the unknown through the known** — define terms, and use concrete visual comparisons for abstract numbers.
- **Motivate the learning** — give the audience a problem they can't solve without the thing you're about to teach them, and say why it matters.
- **Be S-M-A-R-T**: **S**imple (broken down), **M**emorable (visible — seeing is believing), **A**pt (short, brief), **R**eactive (watch the audience's reaction and adjust), **T**rustworthy (be credible).
- **Visual aids, if used**: make them big enough for the farthest person in the room, tell the audience exactly what to focus on, remove the aid once its job is done (put it away, don't leave a stale slide up), expect something to go wrong and have a fallback, don't just read the slide's content aloud, and remember the audience should be watching *you* at least half the time, not the screen.

## Handling questions — the five D's

Use these to keep control of a Q&A without dodging it:

1. **Delay** — take a beat (about 2 seconds) before answering: "Thank you for that question."
2. **Defuse** — if the question is loaded, rephrase it neutrally before answering.
3. **Dissect** — break a multi-part question into addressable pieces.
4. **Depersonalize** — if a question is aimed personally, reframe it to the underlying issue rather than the person.
5. **Deflect** — not every question needs a full answer; it's fine to decline to fully engage one.

One more rule: **never match a hostile questioner's tone or level** — respond with more composure than they brought, not the same energy.

Practical Q&A logistics: if you want questions and none come, either collect them during the talk or plant one yourself ("A question I often get is..."). If there are too many, say so explicitly: "We only have time for one more." Repeat the question before answering it (confirms you heard it right and gets the room's attention), and prepare a few visual aids or facts in reserve for likely follow-ups.
