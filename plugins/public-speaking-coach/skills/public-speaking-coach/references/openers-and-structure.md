# Openers, Structure, and Storytelling Rules

General-purpose tools that apply to almost any speech, regardless of occasion.

## Attention grabbers (pick one to open with)

The first impression sets the trajectory for the entire speech — a strong one buys goodwill that carries through; a weak one means clawing back attention for the next five minutes. Match the grabber to the audience and occasion:

- **The Grabber** — shock the audience or state something unusual straight away. "I'm a..." followed by an unexpected reveal, or doing something the audience doesn't expect.
- **The Curiosity Arousal** — open with an image or idea that makes the audience want to know where it's going. E.g., "Lines. There are lines all around us. Where the wall meets the ceiling, that forms a line..."
- **The Problem to Solve** — state a problem and make the audience feel it's worth solving before you say anything else. E.g., "Millions of people go to therapy — but what happens between the sessions?"
- **The "Woah" Introduction** — a well-built stylistic device or list that stuns, often followed by a turn. E.g., a rapid list building to an unexpected pivot line.
- **The Room Reference** — reference something physically in the room, the occasion itself, or the speaker who just went before you. Low-risk, always available, and shows you're present rather than reciting.

Whatever the grabber, it should also implicitly answer: *why should this audience listen to you, on this topic, right now?* ("By the end of this talk you'll be able to...")

## Patterns of organization (pick one to structure the body)

- **Time Pattern** — past, now, future. Good for change narratives, retrospectives, progress updates.
- **Importance Pattern** — move least-to-most important, or the reverse, depending on whether you need to earn attention first or can lead with your strongest point. Connective tissue matters here: "That matters, but..." / "Which leads us to perhaps the most important point..." / "But even more importantly..."
- **Problem-Solution Pattern** — define the problem, then present the solution. Works for persuasive and business content (see `business-speaking.md` for the fuller version with appeals to values).

For impromptu or short remarks, the same choices apply, just compressed: pick a purpose (entertain, inform, persuade), pick an intro, pick one of the above patterns, use supporting material, and close with either a summary or a callback to the opener.

## The story rules

Any anecdote — personal story, case example, illustrative moment — should pass all of these:

**The ABC test**
- **Apt** — fits this specific audience (adapt it if it doesn't).
- **Brief** — trim it; a story that runs long loses the room.
- **Chronological** — tell it in the order it happened; don't jump around.

**The 3 M's** — a story earns its place in the speech if it's at least one of:
- **Meaningful** — carries a lesson.
- **Memorable** — sticks after the speech ends.
- **Moving** — gives the audience something to reflect on.

**Avoid the 3 D's** — the fastest ways to lose a room mid-story:
- **Digress** — wandering off the main facts.
- **Divert** — introducing a second story before finishing the first.
- **Distract** — breaking the telling with asides; get through it in one pass.

**Building a storyboard**: intro (setting, characters, the conflict) → body (build a climax, then resolve it). Introduce the conflict directly or by foreshadowing — suspense is what keeps people listening.

**Where stories come from**: family, work, school, and stories heard from others. Worth keeping a running "speech file" of these so a fitting one is always on hand.

## Variety — the anti-monotony rule

A speech that leans on one tool for its whole length fatigues the audience, no matter how good that tool is. Deliberately vary across:

- **Voice** (note this for delivery even in a text draft) — speed, volume, pitch.
- **Supporting material** — stories, quotations, metaphors, irony. Don't reuse the same device three times in a row.
- **Requested audience reaction** — laughter, curiosity, reflection. A speech that's all jokes or all gravity reads as one-note.

## Making a point stick: state, demonstrate, restate

For any claim central to the speech:
1. **State** it plainly.
2. **Demonstrate** it using logos (logic, facts, statistics), ethos (a credible source or the speaker's own standing), or pathos (an emotional, human-scale example) — whichever fits the point and audience.
3. **Restate** it, ideally in slightly different words than the first statement so it doesn't feel repetitive.

## Two more delivery devices worth reaching for

**Name the objection before they raise it.** When the audience is likely holding a silent doubt, say it out loud for them before it gets a chance to fester: "Some of you might be thinking, 'won't that just make things worse?' — but..." Naming it does two things at once: it shows you've thought harder about the topic than a one-sided pitch would, and it lets you answer on your own terms instead of getting derailed later. This works in persuasive and business contexts especially — see `business-speaking.md`'s counter-persuasion technique for the fuller version.

**Escalate through a short time-scale progression.** Building a claim through "in seconds... in minutes... in days... in years..." (or a similar escalating scale) turns one static claim into a small narrative arc and makes an abstract benefit feel concrete and inevitable. It's a compact way to add variety and momentum without a full story — useful when there's no anecdote on hand but the underlying point deserves more than a flat statement.

## Closing

- **Summary close** — "And in conclusion..." then sum up the main points. Safe, clear, slightly forgettable.
- **Capsule close** — reference back to the opener and complete the loop. Almost always the stronger choice; it makes the speech feel like one deliberate shape rather than a list that stopped.

End earlier than the audience expects, relative to the allotted time and their likely attention span — never run long hoping to fit in one more point.
