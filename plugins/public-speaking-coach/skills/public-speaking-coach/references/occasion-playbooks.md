# Occasion Playbooks

Audiences carry implicit expectations for these formats — follow the shape, then bring the personality. Straying from the expected shape reads as a mistake, not originality.

## Eulogy

Fundamental rule: never speak ill of the dead.

- Acknowledge the fact of death directly — don't dance around it.
- Help the audience ease into the reality of loss (and, implicitly, their own mortality) without being morbid.
- Move the audience from a present-tense relationship with the deceased to a past-tense one — this is the emotional work a eulogy does.
- Give the audience a way to hold the new relationship going forward.
- Let the audience come face to face with the loss using one or more of: the struggle that preceded death, an emotional detail about the family, or the speaker's own personal feeling. A shared, specific pain brings a room together more than generic sentiment.

## Welcome speech

- Identify the occasion and the purpose of the gathering.
- Welcome the audience — who's welcome, by whom, and why.
- Identify any guest(s) of honor.
- Praise the sponsors/hosts if relevant.
- Build expectations for the program ahead.
- Restate the welcome in closing and wish everyone success.
- Introduce the master of ceremonies or the next speaker.

## Master of Ceremonies

- You are not the main attraction — the goal is "what a great program," not "what a great emcee."
- Make the handoff between speakers as smooth as possible.
- After being introduced, say something brief (about 30 seconds) to establish a relationship with the room before moving things along.
- Have a backup plan for filling time if something goes wrong.
- Don't introduce people who are giving toasts — that's their moment, not yours to frame.
- Stay in charge throughout, including handling disruptions by redirecting attention back to the audience.
- Open the podium to bring attention to the next speaker; close it to signal no more speeches are coming ("Please enjoy your meal — I'll let you know when we're moving on in the program").
- Make it look effortless.

## Invocation / Benediction

- A reflection speech — give the room a moment to slow down together.
- Choose values the whole audience shares even though they're not homogenous: strength, guidance, generosity, and similar universal points.

## Introducing a speaker

Goal: build the next speaker's ethos before they say a word, through ethos, anticipation, and rapport.

- Explain your connection to the speaker or their relevant background.
- Say their name at both the start and the end of the introduction.
- Wait for them to actually arrive at the stage before stepping back.
- Shake their hand, then leave — don't linger.

## Entertainment speech

- The topic still has to be relevant to this specific audience — entertainment doesn't excuse irrelevance.
- Full eye contact throughout.
- Jokes must fit the context; a joke that would land at a bar might not land at a company dinner.

## Toast

- Brief — 60-90 seconds.
- Clever, humorous, insightful, or creative — pick one lane rather than trying for all four.
- Be loud enough to carry, since a toast is usually given from a seat or standing in place, not from a podium/mic.

## Roast

- Has to stay in good fun — never genuinely attack the person.
- Usually built around a single point, not a list of jabs.
- Can playfully challenge what the audience believes about the person.
- Close by shaking their hand — signal it was affection, not an attack.

## Presenting an award

- State why the presentation is being made.
- Identify the recipient and explain why they're receiving it.
- Explain how/why they were selected.
- Note the presenter's own satisfaction in giving the award.
- Describe the award/gift and why it was chosen.
- Welcome the recipient up and present it.

## Accepting an award

- Express appreciation, briefly — don't overdo it.
- Praise whoever/whatever bestowed the award.
- Give credit to others who contributed — but don't turn it into a long list.
- Say how the award is meaningful and how it will drive future effort.

## Graduation speech

- Context is everything: the occasion is that everybody has gathered to reflect on shared achievements and shared experience.
- Don't speak about a purely theoretical topic — ground it in what this specific group actually went through together.

## Day-of checklist (any formal occasion)

- Stand at the podium before other guests arrive to get a feel for the room.
- Check the mic.
- Check any visual aids.
